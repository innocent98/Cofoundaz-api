Your price is not decided by your costs. It is decided by what the customer compares you to.

## Find the alternative

Every customer already solves the problem somehow. The alternative might be a competitor, a spreadsheet, a part time assistant or doing nothing and living with the loss. Ask your interviewees: "What do you do today, and what does it cost you in money and time?"

## Put a number on it

Write down three figures:

- **The alternative's price.** For example, an assistant who handles bookings costs ₦60,000 a month.
- **The cost of the problem.** For example, missed bookings cost the salon about ₦80,000 a month.
- **Your value.** How much of that cost do you remove? If you remove most of the lost bookings, your value to the customer is close to ₦80,000 a month.

## Set the range

A fair first price usually sits at a fraction of the value, often 10 to 30 percent, and below or near the alternative's price. In the example, ₦8,000 to ₦20,000 a month is easy for the customer to say yes to, and it leaves room to grow.

## Beware two traps

- **Pricing from your costs.** If your costs are high but the value is low, the answer is a different business, not a higher price.
- **Pricing too low out of fear.** A very low price attracts people who do not value the problem and tells serious buyers the product is a toy.

Write your range on one line and carry it into the next lesson.
