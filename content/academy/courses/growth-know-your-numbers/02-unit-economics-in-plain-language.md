Unit economics asks a simple question: **do you make money on each customer or sale, before overheads?**

## Pick the unit

The unit depends on your business. It might be one customer per month, one order, one delivery or one subscriber.

## Contribution margin

**Contribution margin = revenue per unit − the cost of serving that unit.**

Example for a delivery startup: a delivery brings in ₦2,500. Rider pay is ₦1,400, fuel and packaging ₦300 and payment fees ₦50. The contribution margin is ₦2,500 − ₦1,750 = **₦750 per delivery**, or 30 percent.

## Fixed costs and break-even

Overheads such as salaries and rent do not change with each unit. Divide them by the contribution margin to find the break-even volume.

If fixed costs are ₦900,000 a month, break-even is 900,000 ÷ 750 = **1,200 deliveries a month**. You now know the target.

## Add customer lifetime

For repeat customers, combine the margin with how long customers stay. A customer who orders four times a month for six months gives 24 deliveries, or ₦18,000 of margin. Compare that with the cost of winning them from the previous course.

## Warning signs

- Negative contribution margin: you lose money on every unit. Growth makes it worse, so fix pricing or costs first.
- High churn: customers leave before you recover the cost of getting them.
- Costs that rise faster than revenue as you scale.

You do not need a spreadsheet model on day one. A table with revenue and cost per unit, updated monthly, is enough.
