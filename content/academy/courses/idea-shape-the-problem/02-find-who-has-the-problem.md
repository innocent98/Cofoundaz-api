A good problem statement is only useful if real people match it. This lesson turns "who" into a list of names.

## Start with people you can reach this week

Build a list of 20 people who fit your statement. Use three sources:

1. **Your own circle.** Friends, family, former colleagues, church or mosque members, classmates. Ask one question: "Who do you know that deals with this?" Introductions work better than cold messages.
2. **Where they gather.** WhatsApp groups, Telegram channels, LinkedIn and X communities, trade associations, market unions, Facebook groups. Read for a week before you say anything. Note the exact words people use to complain.
3. **Where they spend money.** If your people buy something to cope with the problem, such as a notebook system, a freelancer or a workaround tool, find where they buy it.

## Write the list down

Use one row per person: name, how you know them, what they do, how you will reach them. Add a column for how often they feel the problem. A founder with 20 names and a plan to talk to five of them this week is in a far better position than one with a market size slide.

## A warning about friends

Friends tend to be kind, not honest. Use them for introductions, not for your verdict. The people who matter most are strangers who have the problem and no reason to flatter you.
